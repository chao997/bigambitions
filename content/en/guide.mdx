## Follow the Uncle Fred story first

Big Ambitions opens with your character arriving in New York City broke, and the tutorial is built around your Uncle Fred. His storyline hands you your first job, walks you through the city, and eventually pushes you toward your first business decision. The developer reworked this tutorial in Early Access update 0.9 to match the newer systems, so it is genuinely useful — not filler. Follow it even if you usually skip tutorials, because it teaches the map, the job system and the basics of taking shifts.

The story also introduces the campaign structure: objectives that reward you for reaching milestones rather than punishing you for experimenting. If you get stuck, the objectives panel tells you exactly which step you skipped. Most new-player confusion in the first two hours comes from wandering off-script before the game has taught you the map.

## Work jobs, take courses, build a routine

Jobs are how you fund everything early on. Open the map, filter by the job category, walk to an employer and take the shifts — there is no interview minigame, so the loop is quick. Our dedicated [how to get a job](/how-to-get-a-job) page covers the full system, including the difference between working a job yourself and hiring staff for a business you own.

While you work, spend evenings on education courses. Courses unlock the qualifications that better-paying jobs and certain business types require, so the community pattern is simple: work days, study nights, and bank the difference. Sleep at home to close the day — time only advances through actions, and skipping days without a plan is wasted money in rent.

## Save for a real startup budget

Before you sign any lease, price out the business you want: renovation, furniture, appliances, initial stock, staff wages and a marketing budget. A useful community rule of thumb is to hold back enough cash to survive roughly two months of losses while customers discover you. The game will let you open a store with almost nothing in the bank; it simply will not save you when the bills arrive.

Location matters as much as the business itself. Premises in Midtown and Murray Hill sit near higher-wealth customers, which is why community profit threads keep pointing at those districts. Walk the neighborhood before you lease — the customer footfall you see on the street is the one your store will live or die by.

## Pick a first business you can actually run

The community consensus splits the question in two. For a safe first store, simple food and convenience-style retail is forgiving: small stock lists, easy staffing, steady demand. The real money later sits in the so-called Big 4 — jewelry, hair salons, clothing and electronics — but those demand more capital, more staff management and better locations. Our [best business](/best-business) tier list breaks down the full comparison with the profit numbers players actually report.

For your very first opening, prefer a business whose product list you can keep stocked by hand. Delivery logistics and warehouses are powerful, but they are mid-game systems; the campaign will introduce them properly. Layers of automation on top of a store you do not yet understand is how runs quietly bleed out.

## Common beginner mistakes to avoid

Three mistakes account for most failed first stores. First, underspending on the interior: a bare store earns a weak interior score, which drags its appeal — buy the essentials appliances and furniture your store type expects. Second, ignoring bathrooms: food businesses with around 20 or more seats must provide compliant bathrooms, and our [bathroom stall guide](/where-to-buy-bathroom-stall) covers the exact rules. Third, mispricing products: prices you set by gut feeling either starve demand or eat your margin.

The fourth, quieter mistake is hiring nobody. One owner cannot run a register, restock shelves and clean at the same time. Hire the staff your business template suggests, put a manager in charge once you can afford it, and let schedules do the work. The game rewards owners who build a business that runs without them standing in it.

## Money management in the first weeks

Cash discipline in Big Ambitions is daily, not monthly. Rent, wages and loan interest tick out every day, so the number to watch is your daily net — income minus fixed costs — rather than your raw balance. The community's standard advice is to keep the daily net positive before you consider any new obligation, because a single idle day with staff on payroll quietly compounds into a hole.

Banking exists as a tool, and 1.0 expanded it, but treat loans as accelerant rather than fuel. A loan makes sense when it bridges a known, planned expense — a lease deposit on a premises you have already scouted — and makes no sense covering operating losses. Players who borrow to stay open are usually signaling that the store itself needs fixing, not funding.

Also keep a personal buffer separate from your business war chest. The game charges you for food, transport and the occasional night out, and while those costs look trivial next to a lease, they arrive on the days your store is struggling most. Two small wallets, both in the black, is the stable early-game shape.

Once your first store is stable, the mid-game opens up: warehouses that automate restocking, factories that produce your own goods, and premium districts that multiply your margins. Our [warehouse](/warehouse) and [factory](/factory) guides cover that transition step by step.
