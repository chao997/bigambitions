A factory in Big Ambitions is a production building where your business manufactures its own products instead of buying them from wholesalers. You choose a recipe, install the machines that recipe requires, hire workers to run the line, and feed raw materials in one end while finished goods come out the other. Done right, it is the endgame answer to supply costs and wholesale limits; done carelessly, it is the most expensive money pit in the game.

Factories went through several versions on the way to 1.0 — the product progression system made them necessary in Early Access 0.9, a major factory revamp with conveyor belts arrived in 0.10, and the 1.0 release reworked them again. This page explains the system as it stands in 1.0 and keeps the community's honest verdicts about balance in view, because factory profitability is genuinely contested.

## How production works: recipes first, machines second

Every manufacturable product in the game has a recipe, and the recipe determines your entire build. The in-game factory recipe list tells you which machines a product requires, in which sequence, so the process always starts there rather than with buying equipment. A smartphone line, to use the community's favorite example, came out of the later updates requiring only about three machines at a total cost under a million dollars — small enough to fit in a modest warehouse-style building.

Once machines are placed, the flow is materials in, production steps through each machine, finished goods out to your businesses or to storage. Since the conveyor belt update, belts connect stations automatically and may overlap, which removed most of the fiddly logistics of earlier versions. The interior designer and blueprint systems covered in our [store setup](/cinema-layout) guides apply to arranging the building itself.

## Staffing: the cost players underestimate

Machines do not run themselves, and factory staffing is the number that ambushes new industrialists. Every production step needs qualified workers, materials handling needs bodies, and factories run as full operations rather than a register and a shelf-stocker. Community reports on the official forum describe needing on the order of 300 employees for a serious multi-line factory — a scale of payroll most players have never managed in the retail half of the game.

That headcount converts directly into the factory's real economics. Wages are the factory's dominant recurring cost, which is why a line that looks profitable per-unit can still lose money per-day. Model your labor before you build your second line, not after — and remember the [happiness system](/happiness) applies to factory workers like everyone else you employ.

## The balance debate: are factories even worth it?

Here is the honest part: the community has not agreed that factories pay. Critical threads describe production buildings that output too little — one player's full-size factory struggling to sustain a single jewelry store's demand — and scaling discussions where players conclude they need three or four factories just to keep one raw material flowing. Others point to the cheap smartphone line as proof the system works. Both camps are looking at real gameplay; the difference is which products they chose and when.

The reason the debate exists is structural. The product progression system from Early Access 0.9 deliberately restricted wholesale purchasing and introduced shortages and backorders, making self-production the designed solution to supply problems. Factories are therefore best judged as supply insurance and margin control for businesses with heavy, predictable demand — not as standalone profit generators you can point at anything.

## A sane first factory

If you build one anyway, build small and boring. Pick one product your own stores already buy in volume, check its recipe, and size the factory to exactly that demand rather than to the building's maximum capacity. Automate the input side early — our [warehouse](/warehouse) guide covers the storage and delivery systems that keep a production line fed — and put a manager between yourself and the schedules.

Two verification notes for 1.0 specifically. First, the balance numbers above come mostly from late Early Access and early 1.0 community reports; to be confirmed against your own production logs, because Hovgaard Games reworked factories more than once and may again. Second, the roadmap process that produced the conveyor-belt overhaul shows the studio actively iterating here, so treat any fixed "factory meta" as temporary.

## Fitting the factory into your supply chain

A factory should never be a standalone business — it earns its keep inside a pipeline. Upstream, it draws raw materials through your warehouse and delivery network; downstream, its output feeds the stores that were already buying those goods from wholesalers. The production progression system changed the wholesale rules precisely so this pipeline matters: when shelves run short and backorders appear, the factories that exist quietly keep their own stores stocked while competitors wait on imports.

Setting that up well is mostly sequencing. Get the [warehouse](/warehouse) layer running first, confirm your target stores consume the product in predictable volume, and only then size a factory to that measured demand. Players who skip the middle steps wake up to a production line with nowhere to send goods, or stores whose deliveries stopped because a factory monopolized the drivers. The pipeline wins; the lone factory does not.

## Quick troubleshooting

When a line stalls, work the chain in order: raw materials arriving (warehouse stock and delivery drivers), machines staffed at the current hour, belts connected between steps, and storage space for output. Most "my factory stopped" cases are an empty input crate or an unmanned station after a schedule change, not anything exotic. Fix the chain, and the factory resumes being what it should have been from day one: a quiet, machinery-flavored answer to the question of who supplies your empire.

## Choosing your first product

Product choice is where new factory owners lose the most money, so apply three filters before committing machines. One: your own stores must already consume it in volume — a factory feeding real, measured demand cannot strand you with inventory. Two: the recipe's machine chain should be short; the community's smartphone example became famous precisely because a three-machine line under a million dollars is the cheapest industrial education available. Three: the raw materials should be obtainable at scale, since the product progression system limits wholesale purchases and a starved line is a pure wage drain.

If a candidate product fails any of the three filters, park it. The factories that get praised in the community are rarely the ambitious ones — they are the boring, right-sized lines that quietly replaced a wholesaler invoice, month after month.
