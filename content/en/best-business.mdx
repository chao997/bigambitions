The best businesses in Big Ambitions, by community consensus, are the "Big 4": jewelry stores, hair salons, clothing stores and electronics stores. Those four produce the largest steady profits once you can afford them, with electronics stores regularly reported at $16,000–20,000 per day and a big Midtown clothing store cited as the single biggest earner players have built. For a first business, though, the answer is different — simple food and convenience retail is far more forgiving while you learn the game.

This Big Ambitions best business page separates those two questions on purpose, because searching for "best business" usually means one of them. All numbers below come from community profit threads on Reddit and Steam, checked September 2026 against the 1.0 release; treat them as community reports, not official values, since balance patches can shift margins.

## S tier — the Big 4 money makers

Jewelry, hair salons, clothing and electronics earn their reputation for the same reasons: high item prices, healthy per-unit margins and customers with money. One community guide to a first million calls exactly these four the "Big 4" and recommends targeting retail items that sell for about $100 with at least $20 profit per unit. Electronics stores are the crowd favorite for scale, with players reporting $16,000–20,000 of daily profit from a well-run location.

Clothing stores deserve a special mention because the community keeps naming a Midtown clothing store as the biggest money maker in the game. A common expansion pattern is one clothing store per district, so your brand covers the map without cannibalizing its own customers. Hair salons and jewelry round out the tier with strong margins and less stock complexity than big retail.

Location is the multiplier on all of it. M1-type premises in Midtown or Murray Hill put you in front of the wealthiest customer base in the game, and players report big Midtown stores open 24 hours with full marketing reaching around $60,000 per day. The same store in a poor district is a fraction of that, which is why the tier list assumes you can afford good real estate.

## A tier — strong, but harder to love

After the Big 4, several businesses earn solid money with more friction. Fast food restaurants and coffee shops have dependable demand and simple products, but thinner margins and more operational noise: deliveries, seating, cleanliness and the bathroom requirements that hit food businesses with roughly 20 or more seats. Florists, gift shops and bookstores sit in a similar band — profitable in good locations, rarely spectacular.

Gas stations and nightclubs are situational stars. A nightclub is one of the few businesses that prints money at night, but it is staff-heavy and sensitive to how well you manage drinks and entertainment. These are great second-wave businesses once your Big 4 stores fund them, rather than first businesses.

## B tier — fine to start, not where empires end

Convenience stores, fruit and vegetable shops and similar low-capital retail are where most players should actually open their first store. The stock lists are short, the staffing needs are modest, and a bad week will not bankrupt you. That forgiveness is worth more than raw profit when you are still learning how interiors, pricing and marketing interact.

The catch is a low ceiling: none of these become a Midtown clothing store no matter how well you run them. The community pattern is to use a B-tier first store as a money engine, then roll the profits into the S tier. If your first store's daily profit stalls around a few thousand dollars, that is your signal to expand rather than optimize forever.

## The multipliers: staffing, interiors and marketing

Two stores of the same type can earn wildly different amounts, and the difference is rarely the store type — it is the multipliers. Staffing comes first: enough employees to keep registers open and shelves stocked, a schedule that matches your peak hours, and a manager once the payroll justifies it. Understaffed hours are invisible in the ledger except as missing revenue, which is why the community recommends watching one full day of operations before judging a store.

Interiors are the second multiplier. Every store type has furniture and appliances it is expected to offer, and the resulting interior quality feeds both customer appeal and the happiness side of your staff. Buy the essentials fully, decorate honestly rather than minimally, and the store pays you back through reviews and footfall — our [happiness](/happiness) page explains the mechanic underneath.

Marketing is the third. Campaigns raise awareness and pull customers in, and full marketing on a strong Midtown location is part of why the big profit numbers in community threads exist. Spend on marketing when the store can actually serve the traffic it creates; spending to fill a broken store just queues customers for disappointment.

## How to pick your best business in three steps

First, look at your bank account, not the tier list: a jewelry store you cannot stock is worse than a coffee shop you can. Price out renovation, furniture, initial stock, wages and two months of cushion before signing any lease. Second, shortlist by location — walk the district, check the wealth of nearby residents, and remember that M1 premises in Midtown and Murray Hill outearn almost everything else.

Third, match the business to the attention you enjoy. Electronics and clothing reward scale and logistics; salons reward scheduling; food rewards operations. Big Ambitions is a long game, and the best business for you is the one whose daily routine you will not grow to hate. ## Opening-day checklist

Before you cut the ribbon on any store, run the same ten-point check experienced players use. Premises leased in a district with footfall that matches your price point. Renovation complete and interior score at or above the store type's expectations. Essentials appliances and fixtures purchased, including the compliant bathroom if you run a food business near twenty seats. Opening stock delivered and shelved. Prices set deliberately rather than defaulted. Staff hired against the template with a schedule covering peak hours. A manager appointed or a plan to appoint one. Marketing budgeted and running. Enough cash left to survive two slow months. And the address of your nearest furniture store bookmarked for the things you inevitably forgot.

That checklist is deliberately unglamorous — it is the arithmetic underneath every "my store makes $16,000 a day" post on Reddit, and it works for every business type in the tiers above.
